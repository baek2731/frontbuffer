When you buy a digital game, you're paying for a license to play it — not the software itself. IGN's breakdown of digital ownership makes this explicit, and it's the core reason the physical vs. digital debate hasn't settled after a decade of streaming and downloads. The two formats make different promises about access, resale, and longevity. Here's what each actually delivers.

## Physical: What You Own and What That Gets You

A physical game disc gives you something no digital storefront can: access that doesn't depend on a server staying online or an account remaining in good standing. PS3-era games on disc installed directly to the console and ran without a network connection — a design that still holds up when Sony's online infrastructure for that generation goes dark.

The practical advantages of physical ownership:

**Resale and lending.** Physical games have a functioning secondary market. You can sell a game you've finished, lend it to a friend, or buy used copies at a fraction of launch price. Digital purchases have no equivalent — your library is non-transferable.

**No account dependency.** A banned account, forgotten password, or platform closure doesn't affect a disc collection. Digital libraries are tied to a single account; losing access to that account means losing access to every purchase attached to it.

**Collector value.** Limited editions, regional variants, and complete-in-box copies appreciate over time in ways digital licenses cannot. The physical object — packaging, manual, disc — holds value independently of whether the game is still available digitally.

## Digital: Convenience at the Cost of Ownership

Digital games trade ownership for convenience, and the trade is explicit in the terms of service of every major platform. You're acquiring a revocable license, not a transferable asset.

What digital does well:

**Instant access.** No disc swapping, no trips to a store. Pre-loading lets you start playing at launch without a download wait. For large libraries across multiple platforms, having everything accessible from a menu is a real quality-of-life improvement.

**Pricing.** Digital storefronts run frequent sales — Steam, PlayStation Store, and Xbox regularly discount back-catalog titles by 50–90%. For players who aren't interested in resale, buying digitally on sale often costs less than physical over time.

**No physical degradation.** Discs scratch, cases break, and cartridge battery saves die. A digital license doesn't degrade, though it can disappear if a storefront shuts down.

## The Risk That Doesn't Go Away

Digital storefronts have closed before. The PlayStation 3 and Vita stores were nearly shut down in 2021 before user pressure reversed the decision — temporarily, in the case of the PS3 store, which eventually did close for purchases. When a storefront closes, previously purchased games typically remain downloadable for a period, but that window isn't guaranteed, and re-downloading years later depends entirely on the platform holder's continued operation.

Physical games don't have this problem. A disc from 2005 still works in 2026 if the hardware is maintained.

## Who Should Buy Physical, Who Should Buy Digital

**Buy physical if:** You want resale options, you collect, you play older titles where server infrastructure is uncertain, or you share games with others.

**Buy digital if:** Convenience matters more than ownership, you frequently buy on sale, you play on multiple devices under the same account, or storage space for physical media is limited.

The formats aren't mutually exclusive. Most players use both — physical for titles they expect to revisit or resell, digital for games they want immediately or at a steep discount.
